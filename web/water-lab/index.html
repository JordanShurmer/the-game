What you are looking at

The same two things, given to nine shaders

The world reaches the screen as one texture of cells, already shaded by the light. Beside it the game builds a second texture, one byte a texel: 0 where there is no water, and otherwise how many cells of water stand above that one, to a limit of 255. That byte is the mask and the depth at once.

Every plate here gets those two textures and nothing else, under the uniform names the game uses — texture0, mask, size, origin, step_cells, seconds. So each one is a whole data/shaders/water.fs: copy it out, save it over the file, and the game draws that water. The pond under them is drawn here in the browser, from the colours in data/materials.txt, because a shader can only be judged against a picture.

Plate I is the shader in the game today. It is here as the mark to beat. The eight after it are attempts at a new one, each committed to a school of art rather than to realism, because the game is a grid of coloured cells and has no third dimension to be real in.

The other question

What they cost

They cost the same to draw. Every plate is one full-screen pass over the same two textures, drawn with the one call app_draw_water already makes, and every one of them throws away the fragments where the mask reads zero. No plate adds a framebuffer, a second pass, a mipmap or a buffer upload. So the draw count is one, and it is one for all nine.

What differs is the work inside a water fragment. Two things say how much: how many times the shader reads a texture, which is what a memory-bound pass waits on, and how much arithmetic it does between the reads, which is what the table below actually measures. The pass is charged only for the water on screen, so a wide shallow pond costs less than this bench and a screen full of pond costs more.

Two things the table says before you press anything. Plate I searches a three by three block of drifting points, twice over, once for each of the two nets it lays on the bottom; nothing else here searches a neighbourhood more than once. Plate III makes the most reads — twelve of them, two for every brush mark — and reads are what a memory-bound pass waits on. Plates VI and IX read almost nothing and run no noise at all, which is what a demake and a wireframe are.

Taking one into the game

A plate is a file

Open a plate, press Copy, and write it over data/shaders/water.fs. Nothing else moves: the depth map, the uniforms and the draw call are the same for all nine. The loader puts the version line on, which is why no shader here has one.

make game
./bin/the-game shot=shots/water.png frames=140 walk=-40   # the millpond
xvfb-run -a -s "-screen 0 1280x720x24" ./bin/the-game shot=shots/water.png frames=140

A shader that will not compile costs the ripples and not the game: water_load returns on = false, says so once on stderr, and the water draws flat. So a bad plate is a flat pond, not a black window.

What the browser cannot tell you: the game samples the world texture with point filtering at a zoom the player sets, and the light is cast by light_shade over real terrain rather than by the eight lamps in this page. Judge the school of art here. Judge the shader in the window.
